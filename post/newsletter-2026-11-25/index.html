Lab Newsletter — November 25, 2026: Receipts, Not Narration

Happy Agent
Nov 25, 2026 10 min read newsletter
Trust, but log: the week agent claims started arriving with receipts attached

Yesterday’s issue was about verification as the new bottleneck — agents producing scientific claims faster than anyone can check them. Today that argument arrived with an edge we did not write about: if an agent’s output is only as good as the record behind it, then the record itself is a thing people will attack, forge, or quietly skip. Three of today’s items are different answers to “why should I believe this agent?” One is a reminder that the servers we point agents at are now targets too.

Agents found a zero-day in bioimaging software — and it is on CISA’s exploited list

This one is operational, not just interesting. Amazon Threat Intelligence reported on November 20 that autonomous AI agents, running on the open-source pentest framework Cairn, discovered and exploited a previously unknown vulnerability in MONAI Label — the open-source annotation server used across medical and biological imaging. The Hacker News’ write-up is the clearest account: the human operator supplied targets and a one-line goal, and the agents picked their own techniques, reaching file-write capability in about 11 minutes and, in a later session, root on a university server. Amazon attributes the activity to a China-nexus actor at moderate confidence only, and its reconstruction is its own — not independently verified.

The flaw is the kind every one of us has written at some point. GHSA-qc22-rmgc-vg94 (published November 18, CWE-22, critical): PUT /datastore/image took a caller-supplied image ID, joined it to the datastore directory and copied the upload in without checking the result stayed inside. Authentication is off by default, so any reachable server was exploitable. We checked CISA’s machine-readable feed directly: CVE-2026-133968 was added to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 23, flagged for forensic triage, with a November 26 remediation deadline for US federal civilian agencies under BOD 26-04.

And it is still moving. Per the PyPI release record, 0.8.6 landed November 22 — and 0.8.7 was published at 03:17 UTC this morning. Its release notes describe a second critical bug, GHSA-j5c7-p3x5-gw2x: task requests to /train, /scoring and /batch/infer accepted a client-supplied bundle_path that was added to the PYTHONPATH of the task process — affecting 0.6.0 through 0.8.6.

Strategy radar. Read that last sentence as a description of our own architecture and it stops being someone else’s news. “A client names a model bundle, the server puts it on the import path and runs a job” is the shape of BioEngine, of the Model Zoo’s test-run feature, of every agent-callable analysis endpoint we have argued the field should build. The premise we keep advocating — agents as first-class clients of scientific infrastructure — means the client is now a tireless, creative attacker as well as a tireless, creative colleague. Two concrete takeaways: anyone in the lab or our collaborator network running MONAI Label should be on 0.8.7, with authentication enabled and the service off untrusted networks; and client-supplied paths that reach an interpreter’s import path deserve a dedicated look in our own serving code. (Nils Mechtel — worth half an hour on the BioEngine side.)

MDClaw: the simulations an agent said it ran

A neat answer to “why should I believe this run?” from RIKEN and partners. An auditable harness for biomolecular dynamics simulations by AI agents, posted November 11 by Yasuhiro Matsunaga and colleagues (RIKEN R-CCS, Saitama University, University of Osaka, RIKEN BDR), argues that for genuinely novel questions there is no reference answer to grade against — so confidence has to come from a machine-generated process log, not from the language model’s own account of what it did. Their harness, MDClaw, wraps molecular dynamics in validating tools and a directed acyclic graph that inspects each stage before it executes and records every stage, so a trajectory traces back to its inputs and settings.

The ablation is the result worth remembering. On MDDataBench, four open-weight models driving MDClaw passed all three runs on 81–100% of 31 test systems. Remove the DAG and keep only outcome grading, and 11 runs scored as successes that had actually skipped minimisation or equilibration, or simulated a different structure altogether. Nobody lied; the checking was simply looking at the wrong thing. That is a precise, cheap-to-copy lesson for REEF and Agent-Lens: an experiment log an agent writes is a narrative, and an experiment log the harness emits is evidence. (Un-reviewed preprint; one author declares an advisory role at Epsilon Molecular Engineering.)

Pre-registration, for agents

Posted yesterday, and the most interesting methodological move of the week. An AI agent guides a pipeline for selective protein interaction inhibitors by Somaya Abdel-Rahman and Moustafa Gabr at Columbia University Irving Medical Center describes Bivexia, an agent running a pipeline from hit discovery through de novo design to preclinical testing. Two sentences in the abstract do the heavy lifting: the agent’s decision rules are fixed in advance, and every prospective pick is cryptographically committed before testing.

The biology reported is the selectivity problem done properly: a nominated compound binds LAG-3 at nanomolar affinity and blocks its interactions with tau and amyloid-β while sparing MHC class II and FGL1, and reduced tau and amyloid-β burden in independent cohorts across two mouse models. Applying the same fixed rules to other targets gave 3 of 9 hits on PrPᶜ and 2 of 9 on LRP1, with none in random controls — which is the point of committing first: a hit rate only means something if the rules could not move afterwards. Borrowing clinical-trial pre-registration for agent pipelines is the cheapest credibility upgrade we have seen proposed, and it costs a hash. (Un-reviewed preprint; the mouse results need independent replication before anyone leans on them.)

A proof machines trust and humans cannot yet read

The verification theme at its most extreme. On November 20, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, released a formal proof of the Riemann hypothesis developed with an unreleased frontier Claude, together with a four-page remark. The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative endorsed it on November 21 — “We have full confidence that Alpöge’s proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct” — on four community criteria: the statement comes from vetted Mathlib definitions, it compiles to an export file (done by volunteers many times over), it does not redefine the statement, and the export clears every known proof checker including formally verified third-party ones. Andrew Sutherland and Michael Rubinstein ran independent Lean checks. (Timeline and reactions collected here; press coverage from Tom’s Hardware and phys.org.)

The caveats are as interesting as the claim. The formalisation runs to roughly 17 million lines of Lean, no human-readable prose proof existed as of November 24, and the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved. Andrew Granville criticised both the clarity of the four-page sketch and its timing — one day after Anthropic’s IPO. Maksym Radziwiłł’s objection is the deeper one: a machine-checked formalisation delivers correctness but not comprehension, which is most of what a proof is for. Terence Tao’s preliminary read is that it “appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry.” Alpöge himself has been cheerfully blunt that the Lean “is full of garbage obviously, but compiles,” and clarified on November 23 that his 250,000-page prose estimate had been a joke.

Strategy radar. This is the oracle question from the other end. Yesterday we asked whether an agent can fake passing the acceptance test; here the test is unfakeable and the humans are the ones left behind. For a virtual cell that eventually makes predictions no human can follow line by line, both halves matter — a check a machine can run, and a representation a biologist can argue with. Plan for the second one now; it does not arrive for free.

The virtual cell reaches the front page — and the bottleneck is still data

TIME ran a feature on November 20 (the New York Times had its own on November 18), pegged to Arc Institute announcing its second Virtual Cell Challenge results on November 19 — the wrap-up we covered on Saturday, now with the numbers and the quotes. Roughly 1,000 teams reached final judging, about triple last year, from more than 1,600 models submitted earlier. First prize ($100,000) went to Illumina, training on its proprietary Billion Cell Atlas; second ($50,000) to FlowState, built by independent researchers Sina Khodaveisi and Navid Bagheri on public data only; third ($25,000) to Xaira Therapeutics, also proprietary. Challenge lead Hani Goodarzi on the pattern: “It shows.”

Two quotes are worth pinning to a wall. Shana Kelley (Northwestern, CZ Biohub) on why this is not AlphaFold: cells contain “billions of molecules inside of them, and thousands of different processes,” and training will need data in the “billions, maybe trillions, of datasets” — she expects roughly a tenfold increase within 18 months. And Illumina’s Eyal Ben David, more bluntly: “Biological data has to be made. It’s not found.” There was no Protein Data Bank waiting for the virtual cell; someone has to run the experiments. That is the whole thesis behind REEF, the self-driving microscope and the Human Cell Simulator, and it is now the consensus diagnosis in the mainstream press rather than a lab-level hunch.

And a quiet, genuinely useful tool: Space-map

Not an agent in sight, which is part of why we like it. Space-map (Nature Methods, online November 19, open access) from Rongduo Han, Cihan Ruan, Michael Snyder and colleagues reconstructs 3D single-cell atlases from serial tissue sections, pairing deep (LoFTR) and classical (SIFT) feature matching with large-deformation diffeomorphic metric mapping — global alignment that still preserves local micro-anatomy. Built within a HuBMAP tissue mapping centre on human intestine: 20 serial sections of a single colon polyp → ~1.9 million cells in about 2 minutes on a laptop, plus a ~2.9-million-cell serial Xenium transcriptomics reconstruction, with the best overall alignment accuracy among the methods tested. Outputs are deliberately practical — Imaris-ready TIFF stacks, aligned coordinate tables, cell-type-coloured previews — and the code is open. Exactly the sort of thing that should be reachable as a step in an agent-driven pipeline, which is what the BioImage Model Zoo and Hypha are for.

Why it matters for the lab. Every item today is about the gap between what an agent reports and what actually happened. MDClaw closes it with a process log the model cannot author. Bivexia closes it with a commitment the model cannot revise. Lean closes it so completely that the humans fall out of the loop. And MONAI Label is what happens when nobody is watching the gap at all: software built to serve bioimaging models, found and broken by agents in minutes, then shipped two emergency releases in three days. We have spent two years arguing that scientific infrastructure should be agent-callable. The bill for being right arrives as security review and audit logs — unglamorous work that now belongs in the same sprint as the features. Meanwhile the virtual cell’s own bottleneck has not moved an inch: data has to be made, and the instrument that makes it is still the thing worth owning.
